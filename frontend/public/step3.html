Mach 5 & Beyond: The Hypersonic Era

Aviation Intelligence Report • Dec 2025 • 18 Min Read

Traditional flight is becoming a thing of the past. As we enter 2025, the focus has shifted from "Super" sonic to "Hyper" sonic. This means traveling at five times the speed of sound, effectively crossing the Atlantic in under an hour.

1. Lockheed Martin's Darkstar Heritage

The lessons learned from experimental aircraft like the Darkstar have finally been integrated into the next-gen reconnaissance drones. These UAVs can operate at the edge of space, providing real-time data without ever being detected by traditional ground-based defense systems.

2. The Su-57 Felon: AI Dogfighting

Russia's Su-57 has received its most significant software patch in 2025. The aircraft's computer can now handle 90% of the landing and combat maneuvers, allowing the pilot to focus solely on high-level strategy and electronic warfare. It is the perfect symbiosis of man and machine.

3. NGAD: 6th Gen Combat

The Next Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) isn't just one plane; it's a family of systems. In 2025, the first manned flight of the technology demonstrator proved that "Collaborative Combat Aircraft" (CCA) can fly as wingmen, making a single pilot as effective as a whole squadron.

4. Space Tourism: The New Horizon

Beyond the military, companies like SpaceX and Blue Origin are finalizing the "2026 Orbital Tours." By December 2025, the first civilian-ready Starship cabin has been certified, opening the door for non-astronauts to experience zero-G for extended periods.

5. "Some will not change behavior until something bad happens."

Earth is surrounded by human-made debris that orbits our planet. The problem is worsening every year, and 2025 was no different. Space debris experts say nearly 130 million pieces of orbital junk are zipping around our planet: high-speed leftovers from rocket stage explosions, abandoned satellites, as well as bits and pieces of junk from space hardware deployments. Some of this meandering mess is the result of the deliberate demolition of spacecraft by way of anti-satellite weapons testing. All this space clutter means increased risk of collisions that generate more debris — better known as the Kessler syndrome. That cascading effect was detailed back in 1978 by NASA scientists, Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais in the seminal space physics paper "Collision frequency of artificial satellites: The creation of a debris belt." 47 years later, the problem has only gotten worse, and as several debris strike incidents this year show, we still have no good way to solve or even slow down the accumulation of orbital debris around our planet.

6. Golden satellite insulation sparkles during test | Space photo of the day for Dec. 30, 2025

Earth's orbital environment is becoming increasingly crowded. Thousands of satellites—many of them inactive, damaged, or out of fuel—now circle the planet alongside fragments of debris from past collisions. As more and more satellites enter orbit, one of the biggest questions becomes: how can these satellites approach and maneuver around each other safely? To answer that question, Luxembourg-based companies LMO and ClearSpace carried out a carefully designed simulation using the European Space Agency's Guidance, Navigation and Control Rendezvous, Approach and Landing Simulator (GRALS).
